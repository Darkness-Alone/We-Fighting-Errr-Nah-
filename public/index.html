      What the fight card means
      The numbers
      Each number is a count of news articles in the last 24 hours that report one country doing that thing to the other, as coded by the GDELT project from news worldwide. It is not a count of attacks: one big strike can be reported by hundreds of articles. Use it to compare matchups, not as a casualty count.
      Bar: Talk (blue)
      Words only: statements, accusations, demands, appeals, criticism, meetings and negotiations.
      Bar: Flexin' (yellow)
      Pressure short of shooting: threats and ultimatums, sanctions and embargoes, cutting ties or expelling diplomats, troop and naval movements, blockades, military drills, alerts.
      Bar: Hands (red)
      Physical force: strikes and bombardments, attacks on bases or ships, assassinations, seizures, and clashes.
      Clashes
      Armed forces of the two sides actually firing on each other: firefights, skirmishes, border shootouts, an aircraft or drone being shot down. A one-sided strike counts as "hands" too, but a clash means both sides are engaging.
      An hour
      Hands articles per hour over the last 24 hours. Above about 6 an hour is war-level coverage.
      Heating up / quieter right now
      The last 6 hours are running well above, or well below, the 24-hour pace.
      Main event, co-main, undercard
      Billing order: the hottest matchup is the main event.
      Badge: Talkin' Spicy
      Mostly words. Tension, but nothing physical.
      Badge: Flexin'
      At least a third of the coverage is threats or pressure moves, or the pressure is heavy.
      Badge: Swingin'
      Force is being used: at least a quarter of the coverage is hands, at half an article an hour or more.
      Badge: Full Send
      Sustained, war-level fighting: 6 or more hands articles an hour.
      Badge: Nuclear stakes
      A fight here could go nuclear because both sides have nuclear weapons (US vs Russia, India vs Pakistan), or because a nuclear power is squaring off with a country the US is treaty-bound to defend (Russia vs a NATO member, China vs Taiwan or Japan, North Korea vs South Korea). It does not mean nuclear weapons are in use.
      Badge: Big dog involved
      One side has nuclear weapons (US, Russia, China, UK, France, India, Pakistan, Israel, North Korea), or Iran faces a nuclear power. Serious, but a step below nuclear stakes.
      Badge: Regional
      Neither side has nuclear weapons. Deadly, but less able to spread into a world war.
      Not shown
      Allies bickering (US vs UK, say) and matchups with fewer than 6 articles.

            Receipts

                We Beefin Index blends four ingredients. The Action (35%) is how much of the world's news GDELT codes as one state using force on another, plus how hard the hottest matchup is going. The Chatter (35%) is the share of headlines about fighting, weighted by how bad, across the wider press and the front pages of major outlets. The Big Dogs (20%) is friction between nuclear powers plus nuclear-weapons talk. The Money (10%) is Wall Street's fear gauge and the oil price. A missing ingredient hands its share to the others.

                TheseHandsCon estimates where US military readiness would sit on the DEFCON scale, so a war the US is not part of barely moves it. It is built from three signals: the US or a US ally squaring off with Russia, China or North Korea (the only route to levels 2 and 1); US forces in combat with anyone else (which tops out inside level 3); and nuclear talk in the press. US forces trading fire with any state means level 4 or tighter. The US trading blows with a nuclear rival, or a nuclear test anywhere, means level 3 or tighter.

                OSINT scroller posts come from public Telegram channels, some partisan or state-linked. They are shown as they come and never counted in any score: they are fast and often unverified.

                Beef Clock starts from the official Doomsday Clock and moves with the nuclear reading: every 30 points hotter halves the time left. The official clock is set once a year by the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists.

                The index rises fast and falls slowly on purpose, and a status only relaxes once the index is 2 points clear of the line, so it does not flicker at a boundary. Every curve and band was set against GDELT's archive of real days:
